Introduction

My name is Tanatswa Matashu, and I am a final-year Information Technology student at Ensign College, completing my degree through BYU-Pathway. I study from Kadoma, Zimbabwe — which means I have done most of my coursework in environments where the power might cut out mid-lab and the nearest technical peer is a video call away. That context is not incidental to who I am as an IT professional. It is the reason I troubleshoot patiently, work independently, and do not panic when things break.

My technical interests sit at the intersection of networking, cloud infrastructure, cybersecurity, and data. Over the course of my degree I have designed networks in Cisco Packet Tracer, deployed SIEM solutions using Wazuh, written Python scripts for security operations, modeled dimensional databases, and proposed cloud solutions across AWS and Azure. My capstone project — which uses a local barbershop as the scenario organization — brings all of those threads together into a single, integrated IT system design.

I am building toward certifications in networking and cybersecurity, with AZ-900 and CLF-C02 currently in progress. My longer-term goal is to work in the telecom industry, with data science as a parallel track I intend to develop over time. This portfolio is a record of that journey — technical, personal, and still in progress.

Guiding Principles

WEEK 3

Truth

Truth, for me, is not something you inherit — it is something you earn through honest inquiry. I value the kind of thinking that refuses to accept something just because it was handed to me. Whether I am reading a scripture, studying a technical concept, or evaluating a life decision, I want to understand the why behind it. This principle shapes how I approach learning: I do not memorize, I investigate. I believe genuine belief is stronger when it has been questioned and survived. Truth pursued honestly is more valuable than truth simply received.

In my professional life, this principle pushes me to verify before I implement, to read documentation rather than assume, and to acknowledge when I do not know something rather than guess. It is the foundation on which the other two principles rest — without a commitment to truth, clarity and fairness have nothing to stand on.

Doctrine and Covenants 93:24 — "Truth is knowledge of things as they are, and as they were, and as they are to come." Russell M. Nelson, "Christ Is Risen; Faith in Him Will Move Mountains," General Conference, April 2021. Dieter F. Uchtdorf, "What Is Truth?" CES Devotional, January 2013.

Mental Clarity

Mental clarity is the discipline of keeping my thinking clean — uncrowded, focused, and honest with itself. I live in an environment with real distractions: inconsistent power, limited resources, the pressure of studying remotely in a timezone far from my institution. Mental clarity is not a luxury for me; it is a survival skill. It means knowing what matters right now and setting aside what does not.

This principle connects deeply to how I handle uncertainty. I think often about my place in the world, about things beyond my reach, and those thoughts can become heavy. Clarity is what pulls me back — not by denying the weight, but by finding the one next step I can actually take. Professionally, this translates into structured problem-solving: break the system down, isolate the variable, fix what is in scope.

Doctrine and Covenants 9:8 — on studying things out in your mind before seeking confirmation. David A. Bednar, "Gather Together in One All Things in Christ," General Conference, October 2018. 3 Nephi 13:19–22 — on keeping the eye single and avoiding divided attention.

Fairness

Fairness, to me, starts with empathy — the genuine effort to understand where someone else is standing before I decide how to respond to them. I worked in a gaming café, and that job taught me more about people than any classroom. You learn quickly that people carry things you cannot see, and that how you treat someone in a frustrated moment either builds trust or breaks it permanently.

In technology, fairness matters too. Systems built without empathy exclude people. Security policies written without fairness punish the wrong people. I want to build things — and eventually lead teams — in a way that reflects the understanding that people on the other end of a system are not data points. They are people with pressures and contexts I may never fully see.

Mosiah 18:8–9 — on bearing one another's burdens and mourning with those who mourn. Dieter F. Uchtdorf, "Forget Me Not," General Conference, October 2011. Matthew 7:12 — the Golden Rule as a foundation for empathy in both personal and professional life.
WEEK 4 — EXPANSION

Personal Reflections on the Principles

When I first named truth, mental clarity, and fairness as my guiding principles, they felt like values I had arrived at through reasoning. The longer I sit with them, the more I realize they are also values I arrived at through experience — through what it cost me not to have them, and through watching what happens to people and systems when those things are absent.

Truth has shown up most clearly in my technical work. In IT, the system does not care what you believe about it. A misconfigured firewall rule does not behave differently because you assumed it was correct. That kind of ruthless feedback loop — where reality checks your assumptions constantly — has reinforced something I already suspected: intellectual honesty is not just an ethical stance, it is a practical necessity.

Mental clarity became personal when I started studying remotely from Kadoma. There were sessions where the power cut mid-lab and I had to decide, in that moment, whether to spiral or to restart. The ability to narrow your focus to the one thing you can still control — that is not a technique I learned from a textbook. It is something I built out of necessity, one interrupted session at a time.

Fairness is the principle I find hardest to maintain under pressure. It is easy to treat people fairly when nothing is at stake. The real test is when you are tired, or behind, or frustrated — and someone on the other side of a situation needs you to slow down and see their context. Working in a gaming café, I failed that test sometimes. But I also learned from failing it.

Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28 — on agency, initiative, and not waiting to be commanded in all things. Joseph B. Wirthlin, "Come What May, and Love It," General Conference, October 2008. Jeffrey R. Holland, "Be Not Afraid, Only Believe," General Conference, October 2015.
WEEK 4

Professional–Spiritual Alignment

The longer I study IT, the more I notice that the skills I am building professionally are not separate from the values I am developing personally. They are the same thing showing up in different contexts.

My work in cybersecurity — deploying SIEM systems, configuring alert rules, analyzing logs — is fundamentally an exercise in truth-seeking. You are looking at data and trying to understand what actually happened, not what you hoped happened or what the surface suggests. That requires the same intellectual discipline my principle of truth demands: follow the evidence, question your assumptions, do not close an investigation before it is finished.

Mental clarity maps directly onto how I approach technical problem-solving. When a system fails, the instinct is to throw solutions at it until something sticks. The better approach is to isolate, test, and document. Break the problem into the smallest piece you can actually examine. That structured approach is mental clarity applied technically.

Fairness shows up in how I think about the systems I build and who they serve. A security policy that makes the system safer for the organization but impossible to use for the employee is not actually a good security policy. It just moves the vulnerability somewhere else. Designing with fairness in mind — thinking about the person on the other end of every decision — is not a soft skill that sits alongside technical work. It is part of what makes technical work effective.

Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28 talks about being "anxiously engaged in a good cause" without waiting to be commanded in every detail. That verse speaks directly to how I want to approach my career. I do not want to be the kind of professional who waits for a ticket before noticing a problem. I want to be the kind who sees what needs doing and does it — because my values, not just my job description, tell me to.

WEEK 4

Project Refinement Report

SIEM Deployment & Linux Hardening - IT370 → IT497

Project Overview

The original IT370 project involved deploying Wazuh 4.7.5 as a SIEM solution on a hardened Ubuntu Linux environment. Core deliverables included configuring log ingestion from monitored endpoints, defining alert rules for common threat patterns, and building a basic security event dashboard.

Identified Challenges

Alert rule specificity. The original configuration relied heavily on default Wazuh rules, generating significant noise. The challenge was tuning the rule set to reduce false positives without suppressing genuine detections.

Log retention and data lifecycle. The original deployment had no defined log retention policy — logs were stored indefinitely, increasing liability and risk surface.

Access control. The original configuration did not implement role-based access controls on the Wazuh dashboard, meaning all users had equal visibility into sensitive security event data.

Ethical Considerations

A SIEM ingests logs from across an organization's infrastructure — and those logs often contain personal data: usernames, IP addresses, file access records, authentication attempts. Three ethical considerations were integrated: data minimization (collect only what is necessary), defined retention limits (90-day active, 12-month archive, then deletion), and access accountability (role-based dashboard access tied to legitimate need).

Refinements Made

Alert tuning. Custom Wazuh rules were developed to suppress noise from known maintenance windows while maintaining sensitivity to off-hours access attempts and lateral movement indicators aligned with MITRE ATT&CK (TA0001 Initial Access, TA0003 Persistence).

Log retention policy. Active logs retained 90 days, archived 12 months, then deleted. Documented as a standing procedure in FFB's IT security policy.

Role-based access control. Three tiers defined: Administrator (full access - IT staff only), Analyst (alert review, no raw logs), Read-Only (dashboard summary, owner review).

Data masking. Procedure documented specifying which log fields containing personal data should be masked at ingestion.

Impact

The refined deployment is meaningfully more production-ready. Alert noise is reduced. The retention policy limits liability exposure. Role-based access ensures sensitive data is visible only to those with legitimate need. More broadly, these refinements reflect a shift from asking "does this system detect threats?" to asking "does this system detect threats and handle the data it touches responsibly?" The second question is harder and the right one.

Ethical Dilemma Analysis

Case Study: The 2017 Equifax Data Breach

WEEK 3: ANALYSIS

In 2017, Equifax suffered one of the largest data breaches in history. The personal information of approximately 147 million people was exposed, including names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, and addresses. The breach was traced to a known vulnerability in Apache Struts for which a patch had already been released months earlier. Equifax had been notified and failed to act.

The ethical failures operate on several levels. Most immediately: negligence. Equifax held data on people who had no choice in the matter, credit bureaus collect consumer data as a matter of course. That makes the obligation to protect it fundamental, not just regulatory. They knew the vulnerability existed, had a clear remediation path, and did not take it. That is an ethical failure disguised as a technical one.

The response compounded the failure. Equifax waited six weeks before public disclosure, leaving affected consumers unable to protect themselves. During that window, senior executives sold company stock — raising serious insider trading concerns. The pattern reflected a clear prioritization of institutional self-interest over the people the company was supposed to serve.

The lesson: you are not just responsible for the systems you build. You are responsible to the people those systems affect.

WEEK 4: ALTERNATIVE APPROACH

The essential ethical principles at stake were responsibility, transparency, and respect for persons. An ethically sound alternative would have looked like this: upon receiving notification of the vulnerability, Equifax assigns a dedicated remediation team with a 72-hour patch deadline. Simultaneously, security teams sweep for any existing exploitation. If a breach is discovered, disclosure begins within 24–48 hours, affected consumers notified directly, credit monitoring offered proactively at no cost, and a public statement issued before any executives are briefed on stock implications.

This approach would have produced better outcomes across the board. Consumers would have had time to freeze their credit before attackers could weaponize the stolen data. Equifax's institutional credibility might have survived. The executives who sold stock in the disclosure window would not have faced insider trading exposure. And the organization would have demonstrated that it understood its responsibility as a human one, not just a legal one.

The Equifax case is a useful reminder that ethical failures in IT rarely happen because people do not know what is right. They happen because doing the right thing is inconvenient or embarrassing, and the organization has not built a culture where integrity is non-negotiable. The alternative outlined here is not complicated. It just requires deciding, in advance, that the people whose data you hold matter more than the quarter's earnings report.

Reflective Activity

WEEK 3

There is a version of reflection that looks deliberate, you sit in a designated place, at a designated time, and think designated thoughts. My reflective moments do not work like that. They come when I am walking somewhere and my mind goes quiet enough to hear itself. They come in the middle of a conversation with someone I trust, when something is said that lands differently than expected. They come when I am sitting alone in a room and the noise of everything I am supposed to be doing finally stops long enough for something more honest to surface.

What surfaces in those moments is not always comfortable. I think about my place in this world, not in an abstract, philosophical sense, but in a very specific, personal one. I wonder what I am supposed to become. I look for signs that I am moving in the right direction, and sometimes I cannot find them. I think about my family and the weight of circumstances that feel far beyond what any one person should have to carry, and I find myself wondering when things are going to change.

But I have noticed something. The uncertainty does not go away, but it becomes more manageable when I stop trying to see the whole path at once. I cannot control when my family's circumstances change. I can control whether I finish this degree. That shift, from the overwhelming to the actionable, is what those quiet moments are slowly teaching me.

Professionally, the person who sits with fear and keeps moving anyway is not the same as the person who never felt afraid. Integrity under pressure, whether that pressure is a security incident, a failed lab, or a question I cannot answer, is built in small increments. The discipline that pulls me back from spiraling thoughts is the same discipline that helps me isolate a bug or write a security policy when I would rather do anything else.

WEEK 4

Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28 talks about being anxiously engaged in a good cause without waiting to be commanded in every detail. I came back to that idea this week during one of my quiet moments, sitting alone, the kind of stillness where you stop performing and start actually thinking.

Agency, to me, used to feel like freedom. The older I get, the more it feels like weight. The freedom to choose is inseparable from the responsibility for what you choose. And when you are studying remotely, without a support system nearby, in conditions that are not designed for what you are trying to do, the choices you make about how to spend your time and energy carry real consequences.

What I keep coming back to is this: I did not wait for better conditions before building skills. I built them anyway. That is agency in practice, not the grand, dramatic kind, but the quiet, daily kind. The kind that looks like opening a terminal when the power has just come back on and you have an hour before it might cut again. The kind that looks like finishing the assignment even when everything in your environment says this is too hard.

That is what I want my professional life to look like, too. Not waiting until everything is in place. Acting with what I have, where I am, in the direction of where I want to go. The scripture does not promise that the conditions will be ideal. It just says: be engaged. Keep moving. That is enough.
