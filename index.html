In my professional life, this principle pushes me to verify before I implement, to read documentation rather than assume, and to acknowledge when I do not know something rather than guess. It is the foundation on which the other two principles rest, without a commitment to truth, clarity and fairness have nothing to stand on.

WEEK 4

Project Refinement Report

SIEM Deployment & Linux Hardening - IT370 → IT497

Project Overview

The original IT370 project involved deploying Wazuh 4.7.5 as a SIEM solution on a hardened Ubuntu Linux environment. Core deliverables included configuring log ingestion from monitored endpoints, defining alert rules for common threat patterns, and building a basic security event dashboard.

Status. The original lab was built and tested, then deleted when the course ended. The refinements below are documented decisions. I cannot show them running today.

Identified Challenges

Alert rule specificity. The original configuration relied heavily on default Wazuh rules, generating significant noise. The challenge was tuning the rule set to reduce false positives without suppressing genuine detections.

Log retention and data lifecycle. The original deployment had no defined log retention policy — logs were stored indefinitely, increasing liability and risk surface.

Access control. The original configuration did not implement role-based access controls on the Wazuh dashboard, meaning all users had equal visibility into sensitive security event data.

Ethical Considerations

A SIEM ingests logs from across an organization's infrastructure — and those logs often contain personal data: usernames, IP addresses, file access records, authentication attempts. Three ethical considerations were integrated: data minimization (collect only what is necessary), defined retention limits (90-day active, 12-month archive, then deletion), and access accountability (role-based dashboard access tied to legitimate need).

Refinements Made

Alert tuning. Custom Wazuh rules were developed to suppress noise from known maintenance windows while maintaining sensitivity to off-hours access attempts and lateral movement indicators aligned with MITRE ATT&CK (TA0001 Initial Access, TA0003 Persistence).

Log retention policy. Active logs retained 90 days, archived 12 months, then deleted. Documented as a standing procedure in FFB's IT security policy.

Role-based access control. Three tiers defined: Administrator (full access - IT staff only), Analyst (alert review, no raw logs), Read-Only (dashboard summary, owner review).

Data masking. Procedure documented specifying which log fields containing personal data should be masked at ingestion.

Impact

The refined design is meaningfully more production-ready. Alert noise is reduced. The retention policy limits liability exposure. Role-based access ensures sensitive data is visible only to those with legitimate need. More broadly, these refinements reflect a shift from asking "does this system detect threats?" to asking "does this system detect threats and handle the data it touches responsibly?" The second question is harder and the right one.

Ethical Dilemma Analysis

Case Study: The 2017 Equifax Data Breach

WEEK 3: ANALYSIS

In 2017, Equifax suffered one of the largest data breaches in history. The personal information of approximately 147 million people was exposed, including names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, and addresses. The breach was traced to a known vulnerability in Apache Struts for which a patch had already been released months earlier. Equifax had been notified and failed to act.

The ethical failures operate on several levels. Most immediately: negligence. Equifax held data on people who had no choice in the matter, credit bureaus collect consumer data as a matter of course. That makes the obligation to protect it fundamental, not just regulatory. They knew the vulnerability existed, had a clear remediation path, and did not take it. That is an ethical failure disguised as a technical one.

The response compounded the failure. Equifax waited six weeks before public disclosure, leaving affected consumers unable to protect themselves. During that window, senior executives sold company stock — raising serious insider trading concerns. The pattern reflected a clear prioritization of institutional self-interest over the people the company was supposed to serve.

The lesson: you are not just responsible for the systems you build. You are responsible to the people those systems affect.

WEEK 4: ALTERNATIVE APPROACH

The essential ethical principles at stake were responsibility, transparency, and respect for persons. An ethically sound alternative would have looked like this: upon receiving notification of the vulnerability, Equifax assigns a dedicated remediation team with a 72-hour patch deadline. Simultaneously, security teams sweep for any existing exploitation. If a breach is discovered, disclosure begins within 24–48 hours, affected consumers notified directly, credit monitoring offered proactively at no cost, and a public statement issued before any executives are briefed on stock implications.

This approach would have produced better outcomes across the board. Consumers would have had time to freeze their credit before attackers could weaponize the stolen data. Equifax's institutional credibility might have survived. The executives who sold stock in the disclosure window would not have faced insider trading exposure. And the organization would have demonstrated that it understood its responsibility as a human one, not just a legal one.

The Equifax case is a useful reminder that ethical failures in IT rarely happen because people do not know what is right. They happen because doing the right thing is inconvenient or embarrassing, and the organization has not built a culture where integrity is non-negotiable. The alternative outlined here is not complicated. It just requires deciding, in advance, that the people whose data you hold matter more than the quarter's earnings report.

Usability Testing and Design Enhancement Report

Testing method

I tested the live GitHub Pages version of my portfolio myself, in a web browser. I did not recruit other testers. This is a limitation, because I already know where everything is and may not notice what confuses a new visitor.

Issue found: changes not showing on the live site

I replaced hyphens with commas in the text. The change appeared in the repository and in the GitHub editor, but the live page still showed hyphens, and the changes on the other pages did not appear. I first suspected the file had been cut off when I edited it locally. The real cause was browser caching, and clearing the cached files fixed it. Existing strengths

Reviewing the source code, I confirmed that the page already declares a mobile viewport and a language. It has a mobile layout below 640 pixels and a visible focus outline on links and buttons for keyboard users. Animation is turned off for people who prefer reduced motion, and the navy and gold colors have strong contrast against each other.

Fixes I made

I fixed the issues I found. I added a skip-to-content link and wrapped the content in a main section so keyboard and screen-reader users can bypass the navigation. I corrected the anchor offset so section headings are no longer hidden under the fixed nav bar. On phones, the navigation now stacks and scrolls sideways instead of wrapping over the content. I also added the References section to the nav, increased the smallest text from about 11–13px to about 13–14px, and added a back-to-top link.

Impact and what is still limited

These changes make the site easier to navigate on small screens and with a keyboard, and they make the text easier to read. Because I was the only tester, I may have missed issues a new visitor would find. The site still has no images or video, so my next step is to add one relevant multimedia element and to ask at least one other person to try the site on their phone.

Ethical Dilemma Reflection: Misuse of Administrative Privileges

The dilemma

My Week 2 dilemma was the misuse of administrative privileges. This happens when someone with legitimate access uses it to view, copy, or share information they are not supposed to. I used Edward Snowden's 2013 disclosures about National Security Agency surveillance programs as an example (Greenwald, 2013).

My view

I still think this case is not simple. On one side, Snowden had access because of his position, but that did not give him permission to take and release classified information. On the other side, what he exposed started a serious public discussion about privacy and government power. I do not have to decide whether he was a hero or a criminal to learn from this. What I take from it is that being able to access something does not mean I am allowed to.

How I would handle similar situations

If I work in IT, I will have access to other people's data. Before I use that access, I want to ask, "Am I supposed to access this, and what could happen if I do?" In practice, that means working with least privilege, logging my access, and not looking at data just because I can. If I ever saw something that looked wrong, I would not act alone. I would document what I saw and raise it through the proper channels first.

That last point connects to my warning signs. Thinking I can handle everything alone is how a person talks himself into a bad decision. I also built this thinking into my Week 4 SIEM refinement through role-based access, data masking, and retention limits. Those controls exist so that no one person, including me, has unchecked access.

What this exercise taught me

Ethical problems in IT are usually not dramatic. They are small moments where nobody would notice if I crossed a line. That is why I want my habits settled before I am in that situation.
